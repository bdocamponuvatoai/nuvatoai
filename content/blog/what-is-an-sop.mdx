An SOP is a standard operating procedure. It is a written description of how one job in your business gets done, step by step, so the result does not depend on who does it.

This post is for owners of businesses of about ten to twenty people in the US, Canada and Argentina. Each section stands on its own, so read the one you need.

## What is an SOP?

An SOP covers one job. How an invoice gets sent. How a new customer is set up. How a service call is closed out. How a quote is checked before it leaves the building.

It says who starts the job, what they need in hand, what happens in order, and how anyone can tell the job is finished. It is written so that a capable person who has never done the job can do it correctly on the first try.

The word that matters is one. An SOP is a single process written down well. A business has many of them.

## What is an SOP not?

It is not a policy manual. A policy says what the business believes. An SOP says what to do on Tuesday.

It is not an employee handbook. A handbook covers the relationship between the business and the people in it. An SOP covers a task.

It is not a training video library. Videos help someone learn a job once. An SOP is what they open mid-job to check the next step.

It is not a flowchart pinned to a wall. A diagram shows the shape of a process. It often leaves out the detail that goes wrong: which field to fill in, which file to attach, who to ask.

It is not software. A tool can run a process. An SOP is the description of the process the tool runs. You need the description first.

And it is not a document written once and left in a drawer. An SOP is only useful while it matches how the work is done today.

## How do you know a small business needs an SOP?

There is one sign that matters most. Someone is holding the process in their head.

You know the person. If they are out for a week, a job stalls. If they leave, part of the business leaves with them. Everyone else asks them how it works, and they answer from memory.

Other signs point the same way:

The same question gets asked again and again, and the answer changes slightly each time.

Two people do the same job two different ways, and neither is wrong, but the customer notices.

A new hire learns by shadowing someone for as long as it takes, and nobody is sure when they are ready.

The owner is the fallback for every exception, so the owner is in the middle of every job.

A mistake gets fixed, and then happens again a month later, because the fix lived in one person's memory.

A business of ten to twenty people is exactly where this starts to bite. With a handful of people, everyone sees everything. With a few hundred, there may be a department whose job is to write things down. In between, the work outgrows what one person can carry, and nothing has yet replaced the person.

## What does a good SOP contain?

A good SOP is short enough to be read while doing the job. It has the same parts every time, so people know where to look.

A title that names the job in plain words. "Close out a service call" is better than "Field operations procedure."

The purpose, in one sentence. Why the job exists and what a good result looks like.

The owner. One named role, not a team, who is responsible for the SOP being correct.

The trigger. What starts the job: an email, a signed quote, a date on the calendar.

What you need before you begin. The files, logins, forms and information, listed.

The steps, in order, each one a single action written as a command. Open the order. Check the address. Send the confirmation.

The decision points. Where the path splits, say how to choose. If the order is above the approval limit, send it to the owner. If not, continue.

The finish line. How anyone can tell the job is done, and what to record when it is.

The exceptions. The two or three things that go wrong most often, and who to ask when they do.

The date it was last reviewed. A stale date is a warning on its own.

## Which process should you write down first?

Pick the one that has all three of these traits. It repeats. It causes mistakes or delays when it goes wrong. And it depends on one person.

Do not start with the biggest process or the most interesting one. Start with the one that costs you most when the person who knows it is away.

Write it as the job is done today, not as it should be done. The first version records reality. Improving it comes after, once you can see it on one page.

## How do you keep an SOP from going stale?

Give it an owner and a review date. Those two things do the work.

When the job changes, the person doing the job changes the SOP that day. If the SOP says one thing and the work says another, the work is right and the SOP is wrong, so fix the SOP.

Treat a question as a signal. When someone asks how a step works and the SOP does not answer, that is a missing line. Add it.

Keep a single copy in one place that everyone can find. Two versions of the same SOP is worse than none.

## What happens once a process is written down?

Two things become possible.

The first is hand-off. A person who was not there can do the job, and the person who held it in their head is free to do something else.

The second is improvement. A process on one page can be questioned. Which steps are needed? Which ones exist only because of an old habit? Which are repeated by hand and could be run by a system?

Some processes stay with people. Some are better run by an automation or an AI agent. You cannot decide which until the process is written down.
